Module 01

What is Phishing?

Phishing is a cyberattack where criminals disguise themselves as trustworthy entities to trick you into revealing sensitive information like passwords, credit card numbers, or personal data.

🎣

How it works

Attackers send deceptive messages that mimic legitimate organizations — banks, tech companies, or colleagues — to lure victims into clicking malicious links or providing credentials.

🎯

Who is targeted

Anyone with an email address, phone number, or social media account. Employees, executives, students, and individuals are all targets — no one is exempt.

💀

What attackers want

Login credentials, financial data, personal identity info, corporate secrets, or simply to install malware on your device for future exploitation.

Module 02

Types of Phishing Attacks

Phishing isn't just email anymore. Attackers use multiple channels and techniques to reach their targets.

Most Common

📧 Email Phishing

Mass emails impersonating banks, PayPal, Netflix, or Microsoft sent to millions of users simultaneously.

Targeted

🎯 Spear Phishing

Highly personalized attacks targeting specific individuals using their name, role, and company information.

Via SMS

📱 Smishing

Phishing through SMS text messages — fake delivery notifications, bank alerts, or prize claims.

Via Phone

📞 Vishing

Voice phishing — attackers call pretending to be IRS, tech support, or your bank to extract info verbally.

Copycat

🪞 Clone Phishing

An exact copy of a legitimate email you received before, but with links swapped to malicious ones.

High-Value Target

🐋 Whaling

Attacks targeting CEOs, CFOs, and senior executives — often requesting wire transfers or sensitive data.

Module 04

How to Spot Phishing

Train your eye to spot these common warning signs before you click anything.

  • 🔍

    Check the sender's email address carefully

    Look for subtle misspellings: paypal.com vs paypa1.com, arnazon.com vs amazon.com, micros0ft.com vs microsoft.com. Hover over the name to see the real address.

  • ⏰

    Beware of urgent language

    "Act NOW", "24 hours", "immediately", "account suspended" — legitimate organizations rarely pressure you with artificial deadlines.

  • 🔗

    Hover over links before clicking

    Always hover over any link to see the real destination URL at the bottom of your browser. Does it match the organization's official domain?

  • ✉️

    Generic greetings are suspicious

    "Dear Customer", "Dear User", or "Dear Account Holder" — your bank knows your name. Legit emails use it.

  • 📎

    Unexpected attachments

    Never open attachments you weren't expecting, especially .exe, .zip, .docm files. Even PDFs and Word docs can contain malware.

  • 🔒

    Requests for sensitive information

    Legitimate companies will never ask for passwords, OTPs, credit card numbers, or SSNs via email or SMS.

  • 🌐

    Suspicious or mismatched URLs

    paypal-secure-login.com is NOT paypal.com. The real domain is always the part right before the last dot and the top-level domain (.com, .org).

  • ✏️

    Poor grammar and spelling

    Typos, awkward phrasing, and grammatical errors are classic signs of phishing, especially in emails supposedly from professional organizations.

Module 05

Social Engineering Tactics

Attackers exploit basic human psychology — not just technology. Here's how they manipulate your emotions.

⏰ Urgency

Creating time pressure so you act without thinking.

"Your account will be deleted in 2 hours unless you verify now."

👔 Authority

Impersonating trusted figures like bosses, banks, or government agencies.

"This is the IRS. You owe back taxes. Call us immediately."

😨 Fear

Threatening consequences — arrest, account loss, legal action.

"We detected a virus on your computer. Call Microsoft Support now."

🧐 Curiosity

Enticing you with interesting or shocking content to click.

"Someone shared embarrassing photos of you. Click to see."

💰 Greed

Offering rewards, prizes, or money that seems too good to be true.

"You've won a $500 Amazon gift card! Claim it now."
Module 06

Real-World Phishing Cases

These actual incidents show how damaging phishing attacks can be — even to major organizations.

🏢 Google & Facebook — $100 Million Scam

2013–2015
A Lithuanian hacker sent fake invoices to Google and Facebook, impersonating a Taiwanese hardware vendor. Employees transferred over $100 million before the fraud was discovered. This is a classic example of Business Email Compromise (BEC) — a form of spear phishing targeting financial departments.

🏦 PayPal Credential Harvesting Campaign

2019
Millions of PayPal users received convincing emails warning of "unusual activity." The emails linked to a perfect replica of PayPal's login page hosted on a lookalike domain. Thousands entered their credentials, which were immediately stolen by attackers.

🦠 COVID-19 Phishing Wave

2020
The WHO, CDC, and government health agencies were heavily impersonated during the pandemic. Attackers sent emails with "vaccine registration links" and "lockdown updates" that led to credential theft and malware installation. Phishing attacks increased by 600% during this period.
Module 07

How to Protect Yourself

Follow these proven best practices to stay safe from phishing attacks.

1

Enable Two-Factor Authentication (2FA)

Even if attackers steal your password, 2FA prevents them from accessing your account. Use an authenticator app rather than SMS when possible.

2

Verify independently

If you receive a suspicious email from your bank, call them directly using the number on their official website — never the one in the email.

3

Use a password manager

Password managers autofill credentials only on the correct domain. They won't fill your PayPal password on paypa1.com — a built-in phishing defense.

4

Keep software updated

Browser and OS updates often include patches for vulnerabilities exploited by phishing sites. Enable automatic updates.

5

Think before you click

Pause. Breathe. Ask: "Was I expecting this? Does this make sense?" Taking 10 seconds to evaluate can prevent a catastrophic breach.

6

Report suspicious emails

Use your email client's "Report Phishing" button. Forward phishing emails to reportphishing@apwg.org. This helps protect others from the same attack.

7

Use anti-phishing browser extensions

Extensions like Google Safe Browsing, Netcraft, or built-in browser protections can warn you before you visit known phishing sites.
